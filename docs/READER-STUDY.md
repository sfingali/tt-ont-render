# Reader study kit

Status: ready to run; **no participants tested**. Prepared 13 September 2026 for the seven revised editorial guides. This is a formative study, not a statistically representative survey.

## What the study decides

Can someone unfamiliar with the ontology explain the guide’s opening problem, crucial time relationship and ending using the page alone? Can they find the sentence that helped them? Where do they infer an unsupported rule or lose track of a person?

Do not ask whether the ontology is complete. Do not teach time-travel terminology before the session. The guide should do its own explanatory work.

## Participants and allocation

Use five independent readers per guide: 35 guide-reading observations in total. A participant may read at most two guides, with a break between them; balance which title they read first. A practical allocation is 18 people, with 17 reading two guides and one reading one. Do not count repeated attempts by the same person as independent readers of that guide.

Recruit people who have not worked on this repository or its ontology. Record familiarity with each film: unseen, seen but not recently, or recently seen/very familiar. Aim for at least three of each guide’s five readers to be unseen or not recently familiar. Familiar fans can conceal gaps by supplying what the page omitted. Obtain explicit willingness to see full spoilers for the selected title and, for Dark, the stated season scope.

For each guide, include at least two phone sessions and two desktop sessions. The fifth may use either. Record device, browser and any access needs; do not require disclosure of a diagnosis. Include readers who use keyboard or assistive technology if available, while retaining the separate accessibility release checks. Device quotas do not establish accessibility conformance.

No invitations have been sent and no recruitment or payment has been arranged by this review.

## Moderator script

“We are testing a short guide, not your memory or intelligence. It contains full spoilers for [title and scope]. You can stop at any time. Please read it at your own pace and tell me whenever something is unclear. I will ask you to explain it afterwards. You may keep looking at the page, and it is completely fine to say that the guide does not tell you something.”

Confirm spoiler consent before opening the story. Record notes anonymously; request separate consent before recording audio or video. Do not put names or contact details in the repository.

1. Record the guide’s commit or content-file SHA-256, device and prior familiarity. Start from the library so the reader chooses the intended title.
2. Let the participant read without an explanation from the moderator. Record total time descriptively; do not impose an unannounced speed target. After eight minutes, check whether they want to continue or stop. An abandoned session is evidence, not a result to discard silently.
3. Ask the three title-specific questions below, in order, without supplying the answer. Keep the guide available. Record the first answer verbatim before any follow-up.
4. Ask: “Which part of the page led you to that answer?” Then: “What, if anything, is still unclear?” Avoid leading alternatives such as “Was it actually his future self?”
5. Ask the participant to find a relevant connection, then a source note. Record whether they could do so and whether the source’s limit was understood. Do not let source-hunting replace the comprehension questions.
6. Ask for a one-sentence explanation they would give a friend. Record a confidence rating from 1 to 5 and the exact sentence or control that caused hesitation. Confidence supplements the answers; it does not override them.
7. Finish the session before giving corrections. Explain any unresolved factual status. Store the anonymous record and classify findings only afterwards.

If a participant asks the moderator for an answer during reading, say: “Please use what the guide gives you; tell me what you think it means.” Log every substantive hint if one is accidentally supplied. Such answers are assisted and cannot satisfy the unassisted criterion.

## Questions and answer guide

These expectations are based on the editorial drafts. Complete the source checks before treating them as factually approved scoring keys. A participant who identifies a genuine factual flaw must not be scored wrong merely for disagreeing with this document. Accept paraphrases and ordinary language; never demand specific ontology terms.

### Back to the Future

1. What problem does Marty create after reaching 1955?
2. Explain his route from leaving home to coming back. What makes the return possible?
3. What is different when he gets home, and why can he recognise the change?

Expected essentials: the parents’ relationship is endangered; a trip back to 1955 and return to 1985 with Doc’s lightning plan; changed family circumstances contrasted with Marty’s memory. Critical misunderstanding: assuming his return restores everything exactly as before, or treating Doc as a different unrelated person in 1955.

Optional diagnostic after scoring: “Where does the guide introduce the home he originally left?”

### Groundhog Day

1. What prevents Phil from making ordinary progress from one day to the next?
2. What carries over for him, and what repeats around him?
3. What has changed by the ending? What can this guide tell you about the total number of days?

Expected essentials: the same morning repeats; Phil retains memory and learning; a new date eventually arrives after his development, with no specified total. Critical misunderstanding: treating the seven cards as seven repetitions or asserting an explicit mechanical rule that the guide has not established.

Optional diagnostic: “Does the learning card represent one dated incident or a phase?”

### Arrival

1. Where do the scenes with Hannah belong in Louise’s life?
2. How does the encounter with Shang relate to the call during the crisis?
3. What does Louise understand about the family life ahead when she embraces it?

Expected essentials: a future daughter; knowledge from the later encounter informs the earlier call; foreknowledge includes loss as well as joy. Critical misunderstanding: Hannah has already died before the arrival, or Louise physically travels to a reception to retrieve an object.

Optional diagnostic: “Does the time label describe the family’s whole future or the decision in this card?”

### Tenet

1. What is mysterious about the first Oslo encounter?
2. How does the Protagonist’s later journey connect him to that encounter?
3. What connects the masked fighter and the figure Neil pursues?

Expected essentials: unexplained appearances; a later inverted return; the turnstile joins the fighter and runner’s experiences. Critical misunderstanding: unrelated duplicates or an extra fight that replaces the original encounter.

Optional diagnostic: “Explain ‘inverted’ in your own words using this page.”

### Dark

1. How are the missing Mikkel, Michael and Jonas related?
2. How can Michael’s adult life fall before Mikkel’s disappearance on the calendar?
3. Distinguish when Michael leaves his letter from when Jonas learns what it means.

Expected essentials: Mikkel becomes Michael, Jonas’s father; his move to 1986 precedes growing up in his personal experience; the letter/death precedes the disappearance while the discovery follows it. Critical misunderstanding: two counterparts or Jonas already knowing the identity before the disappearance.

Optional diagnostic: ask the participant to use the calendar view and explain what changed from the default reading order. Do not include later-season questions.

### Primer

1. What limits the earliest time Abe can reach using this box?
2. What does he have to do to make the trip?
3. During the overlap, where does the earlier Abe fit in? Is the last card another later calendar step?

Expected essentials: the box’s operating interval; later entry and experienced time inside; an earlier exit overlapping the waiting self. The final card explains that interval. Critical misunderstanding: dialling an arbitrary date or eliminating the earlier Abe simply by returning.

Optional diagnostic: “Point to the part that made the waiting period useful.”

### Predestination

1. Who is the man who disappears from Jane’s life?
2. How does the baby at the orphanage connect to the later agent?
3. What does the final confrontation reveal about the agent’s enemy?

Expected essentials: John as the lover; the agent carries the baby who grows up as Jane; the enemy is an older stage of the agent. Critical misunderstanding: an unrelated child/generation or failure to connect John with the bartender-agent.

Optional diagnostic: “Which name or change of role was hardest to follow?” Do not prompt with an unsupported medical explanation.

## Scoring and decision rule

Score each of the three first answers: **2** = essential relationship explained correctly without help; **1** = partly correct but missing a necessary link; **0** = wrong, unable to answer or dependent on a substantive hint. A successful participant scores at least 5/6, with no critical misunderstanding. Keep verbatim evidence alongside scores.

For each guide, the provisional reader gate requires at least four of five readers to succeed, including at least two from the less-familiar group. Investigate every critical misunderstanding. If a critical confusion remains plausibly caused by the page, revise before passing, even if the numeric threshold is reached. Repeated trouble finding the path, connections or sources is also a revision trigger. A high aggregate across seven titles cannot compensate for one failing guide.

After a material correction, test the revised guide with five fresh readers and record the new revision. Do not pool scores from different versions to manufacture a pass. Minor spelling fixes need no new session unless they change meaning. This small sample can expose usability problems; it cannot support population-level claims such as “80% of people understand the story.”

Mark `readerReview` as passed only after a named reviewer records the actual sessions, checks the evidence and resolves the findings. A passed reader gate does not pass source accuracy, accessibility or deployment checks.
